Opening story

1Why four? And why did Ravi move?

👩‍💼 Priya · Head of Marketing

Last week Arjun showed Priya four customer segments built with K-Means. She was impressed — then asked three questions he couldn't answer: Why four, and not three or six? Would we get the same groups tomorrow? And what can go wrong?

Over the weekend, Arjun reran his notebook with a different random seed, just to see what would happen. On Monday, Priya opened both campaign lists side by side and pointed at a customer, Ravi.

"Last Friday, Ravi was in the Premium group. Today he's in Bargain Shoppers. Which one is he?"

The data hadn't changed at all — only the random starting positions of the centroids had. "If the segments change when I change nothing but a seed," Arjun thought, "then I can't hand them to marketing yet."

This article follows Arjun as he answers all three of Priya's questions and delivers FreshKart's final segmentation.

Quick recap

2What K-Means does and what it promises

K-Means initialises K centroids (randomly chosen data points), then iterates until convergence: assign each point to its nearest centroid, recompute each centroid as the mean of its points.

It minimises the WCSS (inertia), the sum of squared distances from each point to its centroid:

WCSS = Σj=1…K Σxi∈Cj ‖xi − μj‖²
Fact 1

Each iteration can only lower or keep the WCSS, so the algorithm always converges.

Fact 2

It converges to a local minimum, which depends on the starting centroids — not necessarily the best possible one.

FreshKart's dataset: 2,500 customers, 6 scaled behavioural features (clicks, visits, spend, discount usage, days since registration, profile completeness) — the identifier dropped and all features scaled to [0,1], exactly as before.

Reproducing the problem

3Same data, different answers: measuring instability

Running K-Means with K = 4, ten times, using plain random initialisation and a single start each time — changing only the seed.

Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)

Measures how much two clusterings agree. 1.0 = identical clusterings (cluster numbering ignored, since numbers are arbitrary). About 0 = agreement no better than random.

The heatmap shows how much customers move between groups from one run to the next. Values clearly below 1 (the seed-1/seed-3 pair sits around 0.54 agreement with the rest) mean some customers change segment purely because of the random start. This is the initialisation problem: poor starts can leave two centroids in one natural group and none in another.

The brute-force fix

Run K-Means many times from different random starts and keep the run with the lowest inertia. This works — it's what n_init in scikit-learn does — but it's expensive, and a smarter start would waste fewer runs.

Check yourself

Arjun compares two K-Means runs on identical data that differ only in the random start, and gets an ARI of 0.62. What does this mean, and what should he do?

ARI equals 1 only for identical groupings; values well below 1 mean customers are assigned differently depending on the seed. The fix is a better initialisation (K-Means++), several restarts with the lowest-inertia run kept, and a stability check before shipping.

Priya's first question

4How many groups?

K is a hyperparameter — no single number tells you the right K. Three lenses, used together:

Lens 1 · Elbow
Inertia always falls as K grows (zero when every point is its own cluster). Look for the elbow — where adding another cluster stops helping much.
Lens 2 · Silhouette
How well each point fits its own cluster vs. the nearest other one. Look for a peak. Defined for K = 2 to n−1.
Lens 3 · Business sense
Can the business act on this many segments? A 12-cluster solution is useless if marketing can only run four campaigns.

A Dunn index is added as an extra, noisier check — it depends on just two points (closest cluster pair, widest cluster), so it's a secondary signal only.

Inertia always falls with K, so it cannot support K=8, and K=1 gives no segmentation at all. The elbow, the silhouette peak and business sense all point to K=3, with the fourth cluster adding no different action.

Priya's second question

5Can we start better? K-Means++

Random starts cause trouble when two centroids land in the same natural group. What if we spread out the starting centroids?

First idea: always pick the farthest point

(1) Choose the first centroid at random. (2) Choose the next centroid as the point farthest from the existing ones. (3) Repeat.

The trap

This spreads centroids out, but the farthest point is very often an outlier. A centroid placed on a lone outlier may end up as a cluster of one customer.

K-Means++: favour far points, but don't insist

The fix is to pick centroids probabilistically — making far points more likely, but never certain.

  1. Pick the first centroid uniformly at random from the data.
  2. For every point x, compute D(x), its distance to the nearest centroid chosen so far.
  3. Pick the next centroid at random, where a point x is chosen with probability:
P(x) = D(x)² ÷ Σx′ D(x′)²

Repeat steps 2–3 until K centroids are chosen, then run normal Lloyd's iterations.

Interactive: selection probability from squared distance
Three candidate points with distances 1, 2 and 3 to the nearest chosen centroid
Spreads centroids
Points far from existing centroids have large D(x)², so they're much more likely to be chosen.
Avoids duplicate centroids
Points close to an existing centroid have small D(x)², so they're rarely chosen again.
Resists (not immune to) outliers
A single outlier has only a small chance of being picked — unlike the farthest-point rule. Extreme outliers remain a risk; handle them before clustering.

K-Means++ also carries a theoretical guarantee: its starting solution is, in expectation, within a factor of order log K of the optimal one.

Check yourself

K-Means++ has chosen one centroid. Candidates A, B, C have distances 1, 2 and 3 to their nearest chosen centroid. What are the probabilities each is picked next?

Probability is proportional to the squared distance: 1, 4 and 9, which sum to 14. So A has 1/14, B has 4/14, C has 9/14. The farthest point is favoured strongly, but never guaranteed.

Over 100 single starts, K-Means++ has a higher share of runs near the best inertia and a lower mean and worst case than random initialisation. In scikit-learn, init="k-means++" is already the default, with n_init controlling the number of restarts.

Scaling to millions

7How expensive is K-Means?

Each Lloyd iteration: assign (distance from every point to every centroid: n × k × d work) and update (average each cluster's points: ~n × d work). Over i iterations:

O(n · k · d · i)

where n = points, k = clusters, d = dimensions, i = iterations. Since k and i are usually small, time behaves linearly in n. K-Means++ adds about O(n·k·d) for its starting centroids — small next to the iterations. This is why K-Means scales to millions of customers where t-SNE does not.

Runtime vs. number of points
k=5, d=10, 10 iterations · doubling n roughly doubles the time

Measured: doubling n multiplies the time by roughly 1.8–2.2× — consistent with linear growth (small deviations are normal, from fixed overhead and multi-core optimisations in real implementations).

Check yourself

A K-Means job on 100,000 customers with 10 features takes 20 seconds. Keeping K and iterations fixed, roughly how long on 200,000 customers with 20 features?

Time is proportional to n·k·d·i. Doubling n doubles the work, and doubling d doubles it again — about 4× longer, roughly 80 seconds.

Priya's third question

8Where does K-Means break?

K-Means with the best initialisation is still bound by what its objective wants: clusters that are compact, round, and similar in size and spread. On synthetic data where the true groups are known, these failure modes show up clearly:

One big, spread-out cluster next to two small, tight ones. K-Means tends to balance cluster sizes — it can carve up the large cluster to "even things out" instead of isolating the small ones, since splitting a large cluster lowers the squared-distance cost.

Tight clusters next to a diffuse one. These are not recovered cleanly — the boundary falls roughly midway between centroids, regardless of how spread out each true group actually is.

Interlocking moons or a ring around a core. K-Means cuts these shapes with a straight line (equidistant from two centroids), so each half contains parts of both true groups. Centroids land in the middle of the data, not on the curves.

The root cause

Because every point is assigned to its nearest centroid and the objective sums squared distances, K-Means can only create convex, roughly round regions — true for K-Means++ as well. Better initialisation fixes bad starts, not a mismatch between the algorithm's assumptions and the data.

Other cautions

Outliers
Can drag a centroid away or become a tiny cluster of their own. Check cluster sizes; inspect or remove outliers first.
K must be chosen
K-Means cannot say "there are no clusters here." It always returns K groups.
Numeric & comparable features
Requires scaling and numeric encodings for every feature used.
Curse of dimensionality
In high dimensions, nearest and farthest points become almost equally far apart — cluster assignments grow less meaningful.
The "over-cluster, then merge" hack

For differing sizes, densities or shapes, one trick is to use a larger K and combine similar clusters afterward. Deciding which pieces to merge is itself hard, especially in many dimensions. Density-based, hierarchical, or mixture-model algorithms were purpose-built for these situations.

Check yourself

FreshKart's customers form two long, curved, interlocking bands. Arjun switches to K-Means++ with n_init=50 and still gets poor clusters. Why?

K-Means++ and restarts fix initialisation (finding a better local minimum of the same objective). The failure comes from the objective and nearest-centroid assignment, which carve space into convex regions. Curved clusters need a different algorithm, or a transform that makes the groups compact.

Wrap-up

10Key takeaways

Local minima
K-Means converges to a local minimum of WCSS, so the result depends on the initial centroids — different starts can give different segments for identical data.
Measuring instability
Use the adjusted Rand index. A trustworthy clustering should be stable across seeds.
Choosing K
Three lenses — the elbow (look for the bend), the silhouette (look for a peak), and business sense. Stability is a useful fourth.
K-Means++
Picks each new centroid with probability proportional to D(x)² — spreads centroids without insisting on the (often outlier) farthest point. Default in scikit-learn; still benefits from several restarts.
Cost
About O(n·k·d·i) — linear in the number of points, so K-Means scales to very large data.
Limitations
K-Means (with or without ++) assumes compact, round clusters of similar size and spread. It struggles with differing sizes, densities, non-globular shapes and outliers, and always returns K groups — even when none exist.
